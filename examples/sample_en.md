# The Bakery on Linden Street

Anna Petrova unlocks the Linden Street Bakery at five o'clock every morning. The bakery belongs to
Oleg Sorokin, a retired ferry captain who hired Anna three years ago and still pays her wages every
Friday in a brown envelope. Anna works for Oleg six days a week, and he trusts her with the keys,
the ovens and the secret rye starter that his grandmother brought from the north.

Oleg is never far away. He sits by the window with his newspaper and his old spaniel, Biscuit, who
sleeps under the table and wakes only when a tray of cinnamon rolls comes out of the oven. Biscuit
is Oleg's dog, and everyone on the street knows that the dog gets the first broken roll of the day.

At seven, Anna's younger brother, Mark Petrov, arrives for coffee before his shift at the
architecture studio across the river. Mark and Anna grew up in the same small flat above a shoe
repair shop, and they still argue like children about whose turn it is to visit their parents.
Mark draws little sketches of the bakery on paper napkins and leaves them on the counter for his
sister.

Lena Ivanova comes in at eight. She has been Anna's closest friend since their first day of
school, when Anna shared half of her sandwich with the new girl from the second floor. Lena now
teaches chemistry at the secondary school on the hill, and she buys two loaves every morning: one
for herself and one for the school caretaker. On Saturdays Lena and Anna walk along the river
together and talk about everything except bread.

This autumn the bakery has a new problem. A chain store opened at the end of the street, and its
manager, Victor Krylov, has been offering free coffee to anyone who shows a receipt from Oleg's
bakery. Oleg calls Victor his rival and refuses to say his name aloud. Anna thinks the free coffee
is terrible and that the customers will come back on their own.

Mark has a different idea. He proposes to paint the bakery window with a large drawing of Biscuit
holding a loaf of rye bread. Lena promises to bring her students to help. Oleg grumbles, folds his
newspaper, and finally agrees, on one condition: the painted dog must look as dignified as the real
one.

By the end of the week the window is finished. Customers stop to take photos, Biscuit sleeps
through all of it, and Anna has to bake an extra batch of rye before noon.
